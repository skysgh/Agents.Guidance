# Registry Conventions

Apply these conventions when creating or maintaining inventories of dependencies, certificates, licences, subscriptions, releases, budgets or other governed records. The accessible explanation in [Registries](../../../humans/reference/catalogues/registries.md) distinguishes enterprise-referred registries from project-produced registries; use that distinction before choosing a record owner or scope.

For the accessible explanation of registries and the delivery dependencies they may govern, read [External Dependencies](../../../humans/reference/catalogues/external-dependencies.md), [Security at Rest Checklist](../../../humans/reference/checklists/security-at-rest.md) and [Security in Transit Checklist](../../../humans/reference/checklists/security-in-transit.md).

Registries may be maintained manually, generated by automation or fed by both. Each registry needs a responsible maintainer, a defined record shape and a clear way to identify stale or unsupported entries. Do not copy an enterprise registry into a project record without preserving its source, effective date, version and exception or divergence treatment.

## Enterprise-referred and project-produced registries

- Enterprise-referred registries provide approved options, shared facts, constraints, patterns, obligations or risks to more than one project. Projects consume them and record the version or effective date relied upon.
- Project-produced registries record what one project or service actually uses, owns, plans, assigns, expires, releases, risks or proves. They do not become enterprise policy merely because they are shared with other teams.
- A project may produce an exception, dependency, risk or decision record in response to an enterprise registry, but it must link back to the enterprise source and identify the authority for the local decision.
- A registry entry is not trustworthy merely because it exists. Validate ownership, authority, provenance, lifecycle state, review date and stale-entry action.

## Approved technology registry

An approved technology registry records technologies the organisation is prepared to support. It is not a list of personal favourites, a vendor catalogue or a sales target.

Each technology entry should record:

- the capability and contexts for which it is approved;
- the owning technical or platform boundary;
- support status, lifecycle state and sunset or retirement signals;
- maintainability, extensibility, security, licensing and supply-chain evidence;
- required skills, training, operating model and support capacity;
- interoperability, portability, data ownership and vendor dependency;
- cost, quota, performance and availability assumptions;
- replacement, migration and exit conditions; and
- the authority, evidence and review date for the approval.

Approval does not make a technology correct for every problem. A team must still show fit for the capability and consequences of the service. An exception must record the reason, alternatives, additional capability the organisation must acquire, responsible owner, support plan and conditions for review or retirement.

Registry governance must be independent of vendor sales incentives. Procurement and vendor material can provide useful evidence, but neither decides architecture alone. Product branding, low-code demonstrations and promises of reduced delivery effort must be weighed against maintainability, custom behaviour, testability, extensibility, portability, data control and the organisation's ability to operate the result over its full life.

For each governed registry, ask:

- Who maintains the records and who acts when one becomes stale?
- What evidence is required before an entry is approved or renewed?
- What event causes review: expiry, upgrade, security notice, ownership change or support loss?
- Where is the replacement, migration or exit decision recorded?
