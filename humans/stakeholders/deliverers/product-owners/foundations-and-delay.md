# Foundations and Delay

Foundations are work that makes an outcome meaningful, safe, testable, deliverable, operable or maintainable. They include boundaries, contracts, identity, authorisation, data protection, testing, delivery, monitoring, recovery, migration and the information needed by support, Operations and Maintenance.

The Product Owner is responsible for making the value and timing of foundation work visible in product decisions. They are not expected to define every technical detail, but they must not allow foundational risk to disappear behind a feature label or another team's backlog.

## Why foundations are product value

A foundation can protect:

- the user's ability to complete or trust the outcome;
- the organisation's legal, financial, safety or reputational position;
- compatibility with existing users, records and connected systems;
- the team's ability to test, release, observe, recover and change the service; and
- the Product Owner's ability to make future decisions without reopening hidden uncertainty.

The value is not always visible in the first screen. It is visible in what the product can reliably promise and in the consequences it prevents.

## Decide whether delay is responsible

When a foundation is not ready, ask:

- What outcome is blocked or weakened?
- What users, stakeholders, systems or deliverables are exposed?
- Is the missing foundation required by law, policy, contract, safety or security?
- What temporary control exists, and who operates it?
- What evidence shows that the temporary arrangement is working?
- Who owns the work, what triggers it and when will capacity exist?
- What is the rollback, fallback, migration or recovery path?

A short delay may be the responsible choice when it prevents an unsafe or untestable release. A staged delivery may also be responsible when the boundary, contract, risk and future work remain visible.

## Distinguish deferral from omission

A deferred foundation has a named responsibility, a place in the design, a reason for delay, an affected risk, a trigger for reconsideration and a credible route to completion. It remains part of the product's known obligation even when it is not in the current release.

An omission is different. It is work that has been ignored, hidden or treated as someone else's problem without a responsible decision. If the omission transfers known cost or risk to future users, developers, operators or maintainers, it is technical theft rather than ordinary technical debt.

## Make a foundation decision visible

Record:

- the foundation and the outcome it protects or enables;
- the decision authority and relevant specialist SMEs;
- the alternatives and consequences considered;
- the temporary controls or restrictions;
- the owner, trigger, evidence and target condition; and
- the effect on release, acceptance, operation, support, maintenance and retirement.

The Product Owner may decide product scope and sequencing within their authority. They must not accept a security, privacy, operational, legal or technical risk on behalf of a specialist authority who has not made that decision.
