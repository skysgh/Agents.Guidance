# Stakeholder Conflict and Authority

Product decisions often bring together people with different needs, authority, knowledge, risks and consequences. The Product Owner helps make the decision possible and orders the resulting outcome. They do not automatically have authority to resolve every disagreement.

## Make the conflict visible

Record the competing views in language each group recognises. Then identify whether the disagreement concerns:

- the meaning of a concept or Domain term;
- a fact, data source or state;
- a policy, legal, contractual or regulatory obligation;
- product value, priority, scope or timing;
- technical feasibility, architecture or dependency;
- security, privacy, accessibility, records or assurance;
- operational, support, maintenance or recovery consequences; or
- who has authority to decide.

Do not turn disagreement into a vague compromise merely to keep delivery moving. A false agreement creates a contract that nobody can responsibly own.

## Find the decision authority

For each conflict, identify:

- the decision that must be made;
- the person, body or role authorised to make it;
- the evidence and specialist advice required;
- the affected users, stakeholders, systems and deliverables;
- the consequence of waiting or choosing each option; and
- the record that will preserve the decision, rationale and review condition.

The Product Owner may decide product priority and scope within delegated authority. The business owner or policy owner may decide organisational meaning or policy. A Domain authority may decide domain rules. Security, privacy, records, Operations, Maintenance, assurance and technical authorities may own conditions within their specialist responsibilities.

The Product Owner should bring those authorities together rather than giving themselves authority by default.

## Distinguish advice from authority

An SME may explain practice without having authority to set policy. A Product Manager may set product direction without owning a regulated decision. An architect may recommend a boundary without being the operational or security authority. A Scrum Master or flow facilitator may expose an impediment without deciding how the product should resolve it.

Record who supplied knowledge, who recommended an option and who authorised the decision. These are often different people.

## Decide with consequences visible

A decision record should state:

- the chosen option and rejected alternatives;
- the purpose, outcome or obligation it serves;
- the assumptions, constraints and evidence used;
- the authority and participants;
- the affected capability, Domain, contract, systems and deliverables;
- the risks, quality consequences and operational conditions;
- any exception, deferral, fallback or migration consequence; and
- when the decision should be reviewed or replaced.

This lets the Product Owner revisit a decision when facts change without forcing the team to reconstruct the reasoning from memory.

## When authority is missing

If no authorised decision-maker is available, record the question as unresolved. State what work can safely continue, what must wait, what temporary control applies and what consequence follows from delay.

Do not treat the most senior person in the meeting, the architect, the Product Owner or the delivery facilitator as the decision authority merely because no one else has arrived. Escalate to the sponsor or governance route that can assign authority.

An unassigned decision is a delivery risk. It is not permission to convert an assumption into a permanent contract.
