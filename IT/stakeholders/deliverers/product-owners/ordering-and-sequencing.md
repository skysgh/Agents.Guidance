# Ordering and Sequencing

The Product Owner orders outcomes and the work needed to reach them. Ordering is not a ranking of visible features alone. It is the decision about which coherent, evidence-producing path should be taken next and what must be ready before that path is safe.

## Order outcomes, not requests

A request may describe a feature, a preferred solution, a local pain or an urgent message. Before ordering it, connect it to:

- the driver or obligation;
- the stakeholder and user groups affected;
- the outcome that should become true;
- the capability and Domain that carry the meaning;
- the functional, quality, transitional and operational requirements;
- the systems, dependencies and deliverables affected; and
- the predicate and evidence that can show the outcome is real.

This prevents the most visible request from becoming the product's accidental architecture or priority rule.

## Choose a valuable path

A useful sequence creates a safe path to a valuable result. Consider:

- value and consequence if the outcome is delayed;
- legal, regulatory, contractual, safety, privacy and security obligations;
- dependencies and the order in which boundaries can become ready;
- uncertainty that should be reduced before committing to construction;
- migration, training, support, operational and maintenance readiness;
- the cost of leaving current users or connected systems in an unsafe state;
- compatibility with existing contracts and records; and
- the evidence needed before release, transition or retirement.

The cheapest next ticket is not necessarily the cheapest next decision. A small clarification, contract, migration rehearsal or operational probe may protect a much larger outcome from rework.

## Sequence foundations with visible value

Foundations are product work when they enable, protect or prove the outcome. Examples include:

- defining a Domain, capability or ownership boundary;
- agreeing an API, event, data or user-facing contract;
- establishing identity, authorisation, audit, privacy or retention controls;
- preparing a testing, delivery, monitoring, recovery or support capability;
- creating a migration, compatibility or rollback path; and
- producing Support, Operational or Maintenance Manuals or Information.

## Handle dependencies honestly

A dependency is part of the ordering decision when the outcome cannot be delivered, trusted, operated or retired without it. Record:

- the supplying and consuming boundary;
- the contract or readiness condition;
- the owner and authority on each side;
- the availability, security, privacy, quality and recovery expectations;
- the failure and fallback behaviour; and
- the evidence required before depending on it.

Do not make a dependency invisible by placing its work in another team's backlog. The Product Owner may not own the other team's delivery, but must make the dependency and its consequence visible and bring the responsible decision-makers together.

## Make sequencing decisions inspectable

For each material ordering decision, capture:

- the outcome or foundation being ordered;
- the alternatives considered;
- the value, risk, obligation or uncertainty driving the choice;
- affected users, stakeholders, systems and deliverables;
- assumptions and unresolved questions;
- the owner of each required decision or dependency; and
- the condition or evidence that would cause the order to change.

This is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It lets the Product Owner revisit a decision when facts change without asking the team to reconstruct the original reasoning from memory.

## Do not trade away the whole life of the product

A release is not complete because its visible feature works. Before ordering release or acceptance, ask whether the path includes what is needed to support, operate, secure, recover, change, migrate and retire the result.

A temporary omission may be deliberate. It becomes irresponsible when its owner, boundary, trigger, affected risk or repayment capacity is missing. A known, unowned transfer of cost and risk to future developers, users or operators is technical theft rather than ordinary technical debt.
